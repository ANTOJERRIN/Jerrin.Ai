# PROJECT 01 — QUICKNEST

## Overview

QuickNest started as an attempt to solve one of the most relatable problems inside a university campus.

Students frequently waste time walking to food courts, waiting in queues and searching for available food options. Existing delivery platforms are expensive for short-distance deliveries inside campuses and are not optimized for university environments.

The idea behind QuickNest was to build a campus-exclusive delivery ecosystem.

Rather than competing with food delivery giants, QuickNest focused on solving hyperlocal logistics.

---

## Core Vision

The goal was to create an application that allows students to order food, snacks and essentials directly from campus stores with minimal delivery time.

The application would eventually expand beyond food into student services.

# PART 3 — LEADERSHIP, COMMUNITY, RESEARCH & STARTUP EVOLUTION

---

# INTRODUCTION

This chapter represents one of the biggest transformations in my engineering journey.

During the early stages of engineering, my focus was primarily on learning technologies, participating in hackathons and improving technical skills.

However, after months of building projects, interacting with different communities and discussing startup ideas, I realized something important.

Learning technology alone is not enough.

Real impact comes from identifying meaningful problems, building strong teams and creating products that solve those problems.

This realization gradually changed the direction of the organization I founded.

Instead of becoming only a learning community, it began evolving into something much larger.

---

# WHY THE ORGANIZATION WAS CREATED

The original objective of the organization was straightforward.

Bring together students who were genuinely interested in technology.

The goal was to create an environment where members could

- learn together,
- build projects together,
- participate in hackathons,
- improve technical skills,
- and eventually help each other grow professionally.

At the beginning, the focus was heavily centered around learning.

This included

- Cloud Computing
- Artificial Intelligence
- Programming
- Development
- Open Source
- Hackathons

The organization successfully brought together students from different backgrounds who shared similar ambitions.

---

# THE FIRST VISION

The first long-term vision was ambitious.

The idea was to develop members' technical skills and eventually transition them into freelancing teams.

The belief behind this strategy was simple.

If members continuously learned together, they could later work together on freelance projects, gain industry experience and generate income while studying.

To support this vision, discussions included building an internal platform that could manage members, projects, collaborations and future freelancing opportunities.

The organization was envisioned as both a learning ecosystem and a launchpad for professional work.

---

# WHY THE FIRST PLAN CHANGED

One of the most valuable lessons in leadership came from observing reality rather than holding on to assumptions.

As the organization grew, it became clear that not every member shared the same long-term goals.

Many members enjoyed learning.

Some enjoyed attending workshops.

Some participated in hackathons.

However, relatively few were genuinely interested in committing to freelancing projects with the level of consistency and responsibility required.

This was not viewed as a failure.

Instead, it became an important validation.

Rather than forcing the organization toward a direction that members did not naturally support, it was better to rethink the strategy.

This realization marked a turning point.

---

# THE PIVOT

Instead of trying to convert the organization into a freelancing collective, a completely new direction emerged.

The organization would become research-driven.

Rather than asking,

"What project should we build next?"

the new question became,

"What important problem should we solve next?"

This single change altered the philosophy of the organization.

Technology was no longer the starting point.

Problems became the starting point.

---

# THE NEW VISION

The current vision is significantly different from the original one.

The organization is now evolving into a startup-oriented research group.

Its purpose is not simply to teach technologies but to systematically discover opportunities for building meaningful products.

The new workflow is expected to follow a structured process.

## Phase 1 — Research

The core team studies industries, markets, emerging technologies, startup ecosystems and user behaviors.

Rather than immediately jumping into development, time is invested in understanding how existing solutions work and where meaningful gaps exist.

---

## Phase 2 — Problem Discovery

The objective is to identify problems that

- affect a large number of people,
- occur frequently,
- remain unsolved,
- or can be solved significantly better using modern technologies such as AI.

Ideas are expected to emerge from research rather than imagination alone.

---

## Phase 3 — Validation

Before building software, assumptions should be validated.

This may involve

- interviewing users,
- competitor analysis,
- surveys,
- prototype testing,
- and market research.

Only after confirming that a genuine problem exists should development begin.

---

## Phase 4 — Product Development

Technology becomes the implementation layer.

Depending on the nature of the problem, different technology stacks can be selected.

The objective is no longer to showcase technical skills but to build products capable of creating measurable value.

---

## Phase 5 — Startup Formation

Products that demonstrate strong validation and user adoption may evolve into independent startups.

Each successful solution has the potential to become its own company while remaining connected to the broader ecosystem established by the organization.

---

# THE ROLE OF THE CORE TEAM

As the organization evolved, the responsibilities of the core team also changed.

The core team is no longer expected to only organize events or coordinate members.

Instead, it functions as a leadership and strategy group.

Its responsibilities include

- researching technologies,
- identifying opportunities,
- evaluating startup ideas,
- validating markets,
- planning products,
- mentoring contributors,
- coordinating development,
- and guiding the long-term vision of the organization.

This represents a transition from operational leadership to strategic leadership.

---

# LEARNING REMAINS IMPORTANT

Although the organization's primary direction has changed, continuous learning remains a foundational principle.

Cloud Computing continues to be one of the primary learning initiatives.

The organization encourages members to strengthen their technical foundations through structured learning while applying those skills to meaningful projects.

Learning is viewed not as the final objective but as preparation for building impactful products.

---

Another important direction involves organizing technical workshops and collaborative sessions.

These activities serve multiple purposes.

They help existing members improve their technical skills.

They attract new talent.

They encourage networking across institutions.

They create opportunities for collaboration on future research and product development.

Knowledge sharing is viewed as an investment in strengthening the overall ecosystem.

---

# CROSS-COLLEGE COLLABORATION

One of the most significant strategic decisions was expanding beyond a single institution.

Rather than limiting participation to one university, the organization began exploring collaborations with students from other colleges.

This approach increases diversity of thought, expands available expertise and creates stronger teams capable of tackling larger challenges.

The objective is to build an ecosystem where talent matters more than institutional boundaries.

---

# STARTUP PHILOSOPHY

A principle that gradually emerged through months of discussion is that startups should never begin with technology alone.

Instead, every startup should begin with a meaningful problem.

The preferred process is

Validation

Prototype

User Feedback
